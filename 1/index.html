Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Collection

CS180 — Project 1

How It Works

Each glass plate holds three single-channel exposures — blue, green, and red, going top to bottom — which, when appropriately layered on top of one another, produce a striking color image. Unfortunately, the plates are stored as intensity values (black and white) and are presented as one vertically stacked, horizontally unaligned bunch. As a result, we must first split the image into its three channels and then combine them into RGB. We must also find a way to properly shift them so that they correctly overlap and avoid producing artifacts in the final result.

Single-Scale Alignment

For smaller images, single-scale alignment is usually enough. This is when we exhaustively shift one color channel over a reference channel (in our case, blue) and calculate, based on some metric, which displacement is best. This is achieved by:

  1. Selecting the channel and the reference channel.
  2. Picking a dx and dy to shift the channel by.
  3. Cropping both images to leave out wrapping defects on the outer edges, based on a given percentage.
  4. Getting a score by comparing the shifted, cropped image against the reference using a specific metric (either L2 or NCC).
  5. Updating the stored displacement if its score is better than the previously stored one.
  6. Repeating across the full range of dx and dy values.

Scoring Metrics: L2 and NCC

There are two scoring metrics we can use, L2 and NCC.

L2, or the L2 norm, is a very simple scoring metric. It essentially compares the difference between the two images in terms of pixel intensities. Here, a lower score is better, since that means there is very little difference between the two images and they are more likely to be properly aligned. This also means we should initialize our score to infinity (completely different) and only update it when we find a lower score (a closer match). However, this metric struggles when comparing the red and blue channels, because there is inherently a large difference between them — making it much easier for the algorithm to get lost in the noise and misalign the image.

NCC, or Normalized Cross-Correlation, is a bit more sophisticated. It first standardizes each image by subtracting the channel's mean value from every pixel in that channel. It then divides these new values by their norm. Finally, it multiplies the two modified channels together and sums the result. Here, a higher score means the images are closer. NCC does a far better job of aligning the blue and red channels because it accounts for the mean and normalizes the values before comparing them. This makes NCC far more attractive as a scoring metric than the simplistic L2 norm.

The Image Pyramid

For larger files, such as the .tif images, a different approach is necessary — let me introduce the image pyramid. The image pyramid is a very useful tool for scanning large images through selective sampling and blurring, without losing accuracy. It works like this:

  1. We begin by applying a Gaussian blur to both the channel and the reference, then sample only every even row and column, discarding the rest. This quickly shrinks the image while preserving its essence through the blur.
  2. We repeat this until we reach the base case, where the image's dimensions are smaller than 100px by 100px.
  3. We then apply the standard single-scale alignment algorithm, since the image is now small enough to scan through many displacements cheaply.
  4. Once we have this displacement, we multiply it by two (to account for the downsampling factor) and pass it to the next level up as the origin of its search — this time only skimming a small range of displacements around that estimate.
  5. We continue this process back up to the top layer, refining and passing the displacement higher, until we obtain our final displacement.

The One Failure: Emir

The one case where this process failed was Emir — the final boss. NCC on raw pixel values fails here because the Emir's brightly colored robe registers very differently across the red and blue channels. The red search acquires a false match at a coarser pyramid level, and that error propagates up the rest of the pyramid. This is the known hard case the assignment mentions; gradient or edge features would be needed to fix it.
